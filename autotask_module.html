Lesson overview

What this module covers

This short, self-paced module helps IT technicians turn everyday ticketing into clean, usable data. It walks through four decisions you make on almost every ticket — how you categorize it, how you document it, whether it should be one ticket or two, and why those choices matter after the ticket closes.

Lesson introduction

Why this module exists

Autotask was set up fast so we could start moving tickets. That worked — but it left us with a lot of different problems filed the same way. We can see that tickets exist; we often can't see what they were. This module fixes that at the source: you.

By the end, you'll categorize, document, and route tickets so the data holds up when someone runs a report, checks a campus's recurring problems, or plans workload.

Quick thought — before you start

If another technician opened one of your tickets six months from now, could they tell exactly what happened without asking you? Keep that question in mind as you go. No answer needed — it's just the lens for the whole module.

BEFORE YOU START
  • You should already have basic experience creating and closing tickets in Autotask.
  • You do not need to create any live tickets during this training.
  • Think about the tickets you normally close on a busy day — you'll be working with situations just like those.
WHAT THIS MODULE IS NOT

This is not training on how to use Autotask. You already know how to work a ticket. The focus is on making the information we already enter more useful and consistent.

Topic 1

Categorizing the work

In Autotask, the issue and sub-issue fields are how the system knows what a ticket was actually about. They drive routing, workflow, and — the part we care about here — reporting. If they're wrong or blank, the ticket still closes, but it disappears from any meaningful report.

Sort by the primary problem

The rule is simple: categorize the ticket by the main reason the technician was called, not by the first thing you noticed or the easiest box to pick.

If the situation is…The primary category is…
A projector powers on but shows no imageHardware / Device
A teacher locked out after failed loginsAccount / Access
A lab has no internet; wall jack shows no linkNetwork / Connectivity
One application crashes on launchSoftware / Application

The decision, in four quick steps

What is the primary problem?→ What actually failed?→ Which Issue fits?→ Which Sub-Issue best describes it?

Keep it recognizable

Don't categorize based on what was easiest to select. Categorize based on what another technician would reasonably pick for the same problem. Consistency is what makes the report add up.

Before you close the ticket

The category chosen when the ticket was created may not match what you actually found. A request might say "Projector isn't working," but after troubleshooting you discover a failed HDMI cable. Take a few seconds before closing to make sure the Issue and Sub-Issue describe the problem you resolved, not the one that was first assumed.

Topic 2

Documenting the ticket

Good documentation answers three questions for whoever reads the ticket next — including future-you, six months from now, with no memory of the visit.

An easy mental template: Problem → Action → Outcome

Problem
What was actually happening?
→
Action
What did you check, change, repair, or replace?
→
Outcome
Resolved, escalated, or left waiting?

Hit those three and the ticket explains itself. That's the whole standard.

Complete vs. incomplete

Incomplete

"Fixed the projector."

Fixed how? Was it the cable, the bulb, the input? Nobody can learn anything from this later.

Complete

"Projector showed no image on any input. Reseated HDMI at wall plate and swapped a failed HDMI cable. Image restored, tested all inputs. Left spare cable with teacher."

Don't put this in a ticket

Useful documentation doesn't mean documenting everything. Never place passwords, authentication codes, or unnecessary sensitive student or staff information in ticket notes. Record what was wrong and what you did — not credentials or private details.

Practice · Activity 2 of 3

Rewrite the weak note

Here's a real-style ticket note that would be useless in a report. Rewrite it so it answers all three questions. As you type, the checklist below lights up.

"Went to room 214. Took care of the computer issue. All good now."
  • States the actual problem (not just "issue" or "all good")
  • Describes the specific action you took
  • Says how it ended (resolved / escalated / waiting)
  • Gives enough detail to understand without asking you

That's a ticket that survives a report.

Notice you didn't write a novel — three clear sentences is plenty. The test is always "could someone act on this without calling me?"

Model answer

"Room 214 desktop wouldn't boot past the login screen — froze every time. Restarted in safe mode, cleared a corrupt user profile, and rebuilt it. Machine boots and logs in normally; confirmed with the teacher before leaving."

Yours doesn't have to match this word-for-word — it just shows the level of detail that answers Problem → Action → Outcome.

Topic 3

When one visit becomes two tickets

Real work doesn't fit neatly in one box. You go out for one thing and find another. The question isn't "can I close this in one ticket?" — it's "will the report make sense if I do?"

The situation

You're called to fix a slow, freezing laptop. While you're there, you notice the classroom's network jack is dead — unrelated to the laptop, and something the teacher hadn't reported yet.

Two problems, two tickets

The laptop is why you were called — that's the primary ticket (Hardware/Performance). The dead jack is a separate problem with a separate cause, a separate fix, and separate reporting value (Network/Connectivity). Folding it into the laptop ticket hides it.

ApproachWhat the report shows later
One ticket: "laptop slow, also fixed jack"A laptop performance issue. The network problem is invisible — if three rooms have dead jacks, no one sees the pattern.
Two tickets: laptop + network jackA laptop issue and a connectivity issue, each countable. Now recurring network problems on that campus actually surface.

But watch the other direction

Multiple symptoms don't always mean multiple tickets

A teacher reports they can't print, can't reach a shared drive, and can't get online. You discover their network cable is unplugged. Three symptoms — but one cause and one fix. That's one ticket.

Compare that with: you plug the cable back in, and while you're there you notice the projector lamp has failed. Different cause, different fix, different reporting value — that becomes a second ticket.

The decision rule

Same cause + same fix = usually one ticket.
Different cause + different fix = usually separate tickets.

Practice · Activity 3 of 3

One ticket or two?

Read each situation and choose. You'll get feedback right away.

You've got the distinction.

It comes down to cause and fix — not how many things you noticed. One cause and one fix stays one ticket; separate causes with separate fixes each earn their own.

Before the check · why this matters

Where your tickets go after they close

Before the final questions, one last idea that ties the module together: every decision from the last three topics feeds the same place — the data the department uses to make decisions. Here's the chain.

Your ticketing choiceWhat it becomes downstream
Correct issue / sub-issueReports that show what's actually breaking, where, and how often
Clear documentationA searchable record — the next tech finds the fix instead of rediscovering it
Splitting multi-issue visitsRecurring problems surface as patterns instead of hiding inside other tickets
Consistent categories across the teamWorkload and staffing decisions based on real numbers, not guesses

The payoff

None of this asks you to do more work at the ticket — it asks you to do the same work in a way that pays off twice: once when the problem's solved, and again every time someone learns from the record you left.

The whole module in four lines

1 · Categorize by the primary problem, using the box another tech would also pick.

2 · Document the problem, the action, and the outcome — enough that no one has to ask you.

3 · Split unrelated problems into separate tickets so patterns stay visible.

4 · Remember the ticket outlives the visit. Clean data is the whole point.
